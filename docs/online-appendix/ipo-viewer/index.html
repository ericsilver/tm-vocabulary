Outcomes on the lead/atypicality plane, by Nice class

Every registration (registration years 1996–2018) has a position. Further right is more leading: the industry's language later moved toward the filing's. Further up is more atypical: the language is unusual for its class. Both are standardized within the class and registration year. Outcome rates colors each cell of the plane by how its registrations fared against the class as a whole; Registrations draws the registrations themselves, colored by the furthest outcome reached. Point at a named mark for its description, the themes the model finds in it, and what became of it. The two charts under the plane plot the same outcome against lead and against atypicality, each with a fitted line.

The two charts as a table

Named marks in this class

Lead and atypicality are the papers’ production scoring (50 global themes, per-filing five-year reference windows), clipped at ±3.5 standard deviations. Outcomes. A registration fails the five-year proof when it carries a dated §8/§71 cancellation at registration age 4.0–8.5 years; this is a property of the mark. SEC reporting (owner name-matched to SEC financial reporting records) and the IPO marker (an 8-A registration or IPO date) are properties of the owner, so one public firm counts once for each of its registrations. The 2018 cohort’s five-year window is fractionally censored. Outcome rates. Cells are half a standard deviation wide (a full standard deviation where the years selected hold fewer than 80,000 registrations) and are computed on every registration of the class. A cell is colored when it holds at least 100 registrations (and, for the two owner outcomes, at least three expected at the class rate). The five-year proof is shown in percentage points against the class rate, the owner outcomes as a multiple of it. Fitted lines. Least squares of the outcome (0 or 1) on the plotted coordinate, over every registration in the years selected; points are half-standard-deviation bins with at least 200 registrations. Registrations. Grey and blue dots are a random sample capped at 22,000 per class; SEC-reporting and IPO-marker dots are complete, and later outcomes draw on top. Named marks. Owners are matched to SEC records by exact name, so a mark held by a subsidiary (Amazon Technologies, Inc.) is not matched to its parent. Marks named in the papers’ figures come first (in class 32, the earliest registered filing under each name). The rest are chosen by rule from the marks that share their first word with a large public owner’s name: the largest owners (by peak annual revenue) for which the class is a main class, the most leading, most lagging and most atypical among the 30 largest, and the largest whose mark was cancelled at the five-year proof. The owner shown is the owner now on record, which for a mark sold after filing is not the applicant. Theme shares are the production model’s reading of the full description; the class columns average the class’s filings over the five calendar years before and after the filing year, where the scoring uses five years to the day.
