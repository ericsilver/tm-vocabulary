\section{Discussion: unusual language and early language matter at different stages}\label{sec:discussion}

Figure~\ref{fig:staged} re-estimates every outcome on one harmonized
sample --- one row per debut owner, scored on that owner's first filing
--- under a single specification with description length held, and puts
the stages on a common scale: the change in each stage's rate, as a
percentage of that rate, for a one-standard-deviation increase in lead
or in atypicality. (The coefficients are tabulated in Supplementary
Material \S S3; magnitudes differ from the section-specific estimates
above.)

Unusual language is penalized at registration and rewarded afterwards.
With description length held, atypical debut filings complete
registration slightly less often --- unlike the decile profile of
Section~\ref{sec:registration}, which does not hold length and rises ---
then survive the five-year proof of continued use more often, survive
the year-ten renewal more often, and reach SEC reporting slightly more
often. Having been early runs the other way: leading applications
complete registration slightly \emph{more} often, then pass the
five-year proof and the year-ten renewal \emph{less} often. Whatever the
five-year proof selects on, the renewal selects on it again rather than
reversing it.

In the financing rows the two measures behave differently. Atypical
debut owners are slightly less likely to have filed a Form~D notice, yet
among owners who raised a round after their debut, atypicality predicts
\emph{reaching} the public market, by about a quarter of that stage's
3.5\% base rate per standard deviation. The comparison is among
survivors, so it cannot separate a reading in which investors pass over
information that is free and public at the moment of filing from
selection on unobservables. Leading owners are markedly less likely to
have filed any Form~D notice. The ladder of Section~\ref{sec:ladder},
which counts only rounds raised \emph{after} the debut filing, shows
funding flat across fifths of lead, so the difference lies in rounds
raised before an owner's first trademark filing --- consistent with the
established, already-funded firms that also dominate the lagging end of
the reporting and listing rungs. Three limits bound these rows:
Regulation~D notices are electronic only from 2009; owners are matched
by normalized name, which succeeds for a minority favouring formally
constituted entities; and the listing row conditions on funding realized
after the filing being scored, so it establishes that information
distinguishing eventual listers was present in the text years earlier,
not that the language caused the outcome.

Lead does not reverse anywhere after registration: it is negative at the
five-year proof and the year-ten renewal, in the Form~D rows, at listing
among the funded, and at SEC reporting among registered owners. Leading
filings fare worse at every stage after the one at which lead could not
yet be observed.

Why would having been early cost? The plainest reading is crowding. A
filing leads because the kind of offering it described became more
common among the firms that filed after it (Figure~\ref{fig:crowding}):
its idea was duplicated, and the competition it faced grew beyond what it
was when the firm filed. A mark that was unusual in 2011 is ordinary by
2016 not because its owner changed anything but because the category
arrived. On this reading the lead penalty is what later entrants
competing away an early position would produce, and atypicality and
lead fit together: atypicality is how far from the crowd a filing
started, lead how quickly the crowd closed the gap. The design cannot
demonstrate that mechanism. The crowding it measures is in the
industry's language, not in market shares, and a filing can sit where
its class was heading because it was imitated, because both it and its
imitators responded to the same outside event, or by coincidence. What
can be said is that a penalty on lead at equal atypicality is what the
mechanism would produce if it were real.

A second reading follows from the first, and the design cannot test it
either. Privately, writing ahead of the industry's language is hard to
rationalize: no stage of the record rewards it, and each era's leading
vocabulary drew filers by the thousands anyway. Socially, the
exploration is not obviously waste. The corpus moves --- what makes a
filing leading is precisely that its class later wrote the way it did
--- so the language every industry eventually standardized on was first
filed by entrants who disproportionately did not survive to use it. If
finding the vocabulary an industry will adopt has value to the eventual
adopters, that value accrued to the followers while the cost fell on the
explorers.
