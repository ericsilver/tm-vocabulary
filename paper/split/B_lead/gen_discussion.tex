\section{Discussion: each axis bites at a different stage}\label{sec:discussion}

Table~\ref{tab:staged} re-estimates every outcome on one harmonized
sample --- one row per debut owner, scored on that owner's first filing
--- under a single specification, so the coefficients can be read down a
column. Magnitudes differ from the section-specific tables above.

\input{results/staged_outcomes_table.tex}

Unusual language is penalized at registration and rewarded afterwards.
With description length held, as it is throughout this table, atypical
debut filings complete registration less often ($-0.98$pp per standard
deviation) --- unlike the decile profile of
Section~\ref{sec:reg_rates}, which does not hold length and rises ---
then survive the five-year proof of continued use more often ($+1.31$pp)
and reach SEC reporting slightly more often ($+0.017$pp on a 0.56\%
base). Having been early runs the other way: leading applications
complete registration slightly \emph{more} often ($+0.47$pp) and then
pass the five-year proof \emph{less} often ($-0.97$pp on passing). Both
survival rows point the same way at the year-ten renewal, four years
later and on about a quarter of the sample: atypicality $+1.38$pp and
lead $-0.43$pp among marks that had already cleared the five-year proof.
Whatever the five-year proof selects on, the renewal selects on it again
rather than reversing it.

In the financing rows the two axes behave differently. Atypical debut
owners raise a Regulation~D round slightly \emph{less} often ($-0.17$pp
per standard deviation against a 2.45\% base), yet among owners who did
raise one, the same measure predicts \emph{reaching} the public market
($+0.87$pp on a 3.51\% base, $t = 6.3$). The comparison is among
survivors, so it cannot separate a reading in which investors pass over
information that is free and public at the moment of filing from
selection on unobservables. Three limits bound these rows: Regulation~D
notices are electronic only from 2009; owners are matched by normalized
name, which succeeds for a minority favouring formally constituted
entities; and the listing row conditions on funding realized after the
filing being scored, so it establishes that information distinguishing
eventual listers was present in the text years earlier, not that the
language caused the outcome.

Lead does not reverse anywhere after registration: negative at the
five-year proof and the year-ten renewal, at financing with or without
registration imposed, at listing among the funded, and at SEC reporting
among registered owners. Leading filings fare worse at every stage after
the one at which lead could not yet be observed. (In the quintile
contrasts of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}, funding is flat across fifths of
lead. The two summaries differ in form: the ladder compares fifths cut
within class, year and length, while this table fits a linear term with
length held continuously.)

Why would having been early cost? The two axes are not independent in the
obvious economic sense. What makes a filing leading is precisely that its
industry moved toward its language, and an industry that has moved toward
your language now describes its goods the way you describe yours. Being
early therefore consumes the quantity that pays: the mark that was
unusual in 2011 is ordinary by 2016 not because its owner changed
anything but because the category arrived. On this reading atypicality is
a position competitors erode, and lead measures how fast. The design
cannot demonstrate that mechanism; what can be said is that a negative
coefficient on lead at equal atypicality is what the mechanism would
produce if it were real.

A second reading follows from the first, and the design cannot test it
either. Privately, writing ahead of the industry's language is hard to
rationalize: no stage of the record rewards it, and each era's leading
vocabulary drew filers by the thousands anyway. Socially, the
exploration is not obviously waste. The corpus moves --- what makes a
filing leading is precisely that its class later wrote the way it did
--- so the language every industry eventually standardized on was first
filed by entrants who disproportionately did not survive to use it. If
finding the vocabulary an industry will adopt has value to the eventual
adopters, that value accrued to the followers while the cost fell on the
explorers.
