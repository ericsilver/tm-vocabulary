\section{The measure and the record in brief}\label{sec:measure}\label{sec:construct}

\subsection{Two readers, one living backwards}

Imagine two readers of a filing's goods/services description, each having
seen a disjoint half of its industry. One has read only what the industry
filed in the five years before it. The other has read only the five years
after. Each is asked how surprising the wording is. The measurement is how
much they disagree: a description that startles the first reader and not
the second arrived before its industry's language moved that way.

\subsection{From a description to two numbers}

The surprise is measured on themes rather than on individual words. A
theme is a list of words that tend to turn up in the same descriptions
--- \emph{video, computer, electronic, music, games}, say, or \emph{store,
retail, store services, featuring}. The lists are not written by hand.
They are found by latent Dirichlet allocation \citep{Blei2003}, a
standard method that starts from a random guess about which words belong
together and adjusts the lists, pass after pass, until each description
can be explained by a few themes and each theme uses a consistent set of
words. The model is fitted once, on 10{,}000 descriptions from each of
the 45 Nice classes, and then applied to every filing, so that each
description becomes a mix of themes that sums to one --- for example 40\%
of one theme and 15\% of another. We use fifty themes, roughly one per
Nice class. That is coarse by design: at fifty every theme's leading
words genuinely occur together, while at 200 and 500 themes a tenth and
a fifth of themes do not \citep{Lau2014}; and the findings below do not
depend on the choice, since the companion paper rescores every filing at
200 and 500 themes and with fifty themes fitted separately inside each
class, and the headline penalty holds under all of them
\citep{SilverCorpus2026}.

Write $P$ for a filing's theme mix, and $Q^{-}$ and $Q^{+}$ for the
average theme mix of every same-class filing in the 1{,}826 days before
and after its filing date. The filing's past-facing and future-facing
surprise are the Kullback--Leibler divergences
$K^{-} = \mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, Q^{-})$ and
$K^{+} = \mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, Q^{+})$, in nats: zero when the filing's
mix matches its class's, and larger the more weight the filing puts
where the class puts little. The two axes used throughout are their
average and their signed difference:
\[
  A \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\left(K^{-} + K^{+}\right) \quad\text{(atypicality)},
  \qquad
  L \;=\; K^{-} - K^{+} \quad\text{(lead)} .
\]
A filing is \emph{leading} when $L > 0$: its language was closer to what
the class wrote afterwards than to what it wrote before. The two
surprises correlate at $0.99$ --- a filing far from its class's past is
almost always far from its future too --- so their average carries the
large shared component, how far from the class the filing sits, and
their difference carries the small residual that holds the timing.
\textsc{Amazon.com}'s 1997 filing in advertising and retail sits at the
47th percentile of its class on atypicality and the 94th on lead: the
class moved toward its description over the following five years and had
not been writing that way in the five before.

Three properties of the construction matter for reading the results.
First, themes rather than words: a divergence measured on words mostly
counts description length, because a short description cannot spread
over many words, and word-scored atypicality correlates with log
distinct terms at $-0.64$; with themes the correlation is between $-0.10$
and $+0.10$. Second, within class rather than across it: descriptions
are drafted from the USPTO's Identification Manual, house precedent and
competitors' filings, so a class's spread of atypicality reflects its
drafting conventions rather than its inventiveness. Every estimate is
taken within class and year, and magnitudes compare across classes only
as signs and orderings. Third, the measure reads which themes a filing
draws on and how heavily, not how it combines them, so novelty assembled
from ordinary parts is close to invisible to it. Nomenclature follows
\citet{Murdock2017} and \citet{Barron2018} in arithmetic and departs from
them in names (Section~\ref{sec:nomenclature}).
