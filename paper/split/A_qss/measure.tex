\section{The measure}\label{sec:measure}\label{sec:construct}

\subsection{Two readers, one living backwards}

Imagine two readers of a filing's goods/services description, each having
seen a disjoint half of its industry. One has read only what the industry
filed in the five years before it. The other, living backwards as Merlin
was said to, has read only the five years after. Each is asked how
surprising the wording is. The measurement is how much they disagree: a
description that startles the first reader and not the second arrived
before its industry's language moved that way.

Both windows are anchored on the filing's own date, spanning the 1,826
days before it and the 1,826 days after, and both exclude the filing
date itself. Each window holds every application filed in the same Nice
class in that span, registered or not, and every one counts the same; a
filing is scored only when each of its windows holds at least 500
filings. The measure asks whether wording is unusual against a period,
not whether it is ahead of a trend inside that period; it has no
momentum channel.

\subsection{What is measured}

The quantity is information content in Shannon's sense. Something that
happens with probability $p$ carries $\log(1/p)$ of information when it
happens: a common thing carries little, a rare thing a lot. Here the
things are the themes a description draws on, and the probabilities are
how often the filing's own industry drew on each of them in the
reference window. A filing's score asks, in effect: how surprising is
this mix of themes to someone who has only seen what the industry files?

The apparatus is that of \citet{Murdock2017}, developed on Darwin's
reading notebooks, and \citet{Barron2018}, who extended it to French
Revolution parliamentary debates; they call the two quantities
\emph{novelty} and \emph{transience} and their signed difference
\emph{resonance}. The arithmetic carries over unchanged; the
interpretation does not, and Section~\ref{sec:related} renames the
quantities for a commercial record in which the author is usually
counsel. One usage note carries through everywhere: in those settings
latent Dirichlet allocation identifies \emph{topics} --- what a document
is about. A goods/services description is not about anything in that
sense; it lists components, and across millions of filers a cluster of
co-occurring terms (\emph{video, music, games, electronic, audio}) is a
component many offerings share. \emph{Theme} is the word used
throughout; \emph{topic} appears only in the model's name.

\subsection{From a description to two numbers}\label{sec:definitions}

\paragraph{Counting words.} A description is reduced to a bag of words:
lowercased, split into tokens of three or more letters, and counted,
with adjacent pairs counted as tokens in their own right so that
\emph{computer software} registers alongside \emph{computer} and
\emph{software}. Take the filing for \textsc{Amazon.com}, lodged in the
advertising and retail class on 18 April 1997:

\begin{quote}\small
computerized on line search and ordering service featuring the wholesale
and retail distribution of books, music, motion pictures, multimedia
products and computer software in the form of printed books,
audiocassettes, videocassettes, compact disks, floppy disks, CD ROMs,
and direct digital transmission
\end{quote}

\noindent Of its 65 distinct words and word pairs, 35 are in the theme
model's vocabulary, among them \emph{computerized}, \emph{search},
\emph{ordering service}, \emph{retail} and \emph{digital transmission}.

\paragraph{Grouping words into themes.} Next, each description is
re-described using a short list of themes instead of tens of thousands of
separate words. A theme here is simply a list of words that tend to turn
up in the same descriptions: \emph{video, computer, electronic, music,
games}, say, or \emph{store, retail, store services, featuring}. We do not
write these lists ourselves. They are found by a standard statistical
method, latent Dirichlet allocation \citep{Blei2003}, which works roughly
as follows. It starts from a random guess about which words belong to
which theme, then passes over the descriptions again and again, each time
adjusting the word lists so that two things become true together: each
description can be explained by a few themes, and each theme uses a
consistent set of words. When the lists stop changing, each theme is a
weighted list of words, and each description is a mix of themes --- for
example 40\% of one, 15\% of another --- that sums to one. Nothing tells
the model what the themes should be about; we name them afterwards by
reading their leading words. The online appendix lists every theme with
its leading words and its weight in each class.\footnote{\onlineappendixnote}

Three choices go into the model, and each affects what it can see.
\emph{What it learns from.} The model is fitted once, on a sample of
10{,}000 descriptions from each of the 45 Nice classes (every description,
for classes smaller than that), 448{,}437 in all, and then applied to all
13.99 million. Sampling each class equally keeps the largest classes ---
software and electronics alone holds 1.8 million filings --- from
dominating the theme list, so that small industries get themes of their
own. The cost is that vocabulary concentrated in the largest classes is
thinned before the model sees it. Descriptions using internet terms are
7.4\% of the sample against 14.7\% of the corpus; for cloud computing the
figures are 0.4\% against 2.4\%, for artificial intelligence 0.4\% against
0.7\%, and for blockchain 0.2\% against 0.5\%, while solar vocabulary,
spread over many small classes, enters at its true rate. Neither the 50-
nor the 500-theme model has a dedicated theme for artificial
intelligence, blockchain or cloud computing, which is why analyses of
those waves use curated word lists rather than themes. \emph{Which words count.} The vocabulary is every word, and every pair
of adjacent words, that appears in at least 50 sampled descriptions,
after common function words such as \emph{the} and \emph{for} are
removed: 62{,}168 terms.\footnote{The fit uses scikit-learn's online
variational implementation with its default priors, eight passes over
the sample, and a fixed random seed; the sample is drawn from filings
dated 1990--2024. When filings are scored, each description is read with
a simpler tokenizer than the one used for fitting (words of three or
more letters, function words kept, word pairs formed across
punctuation), so a word pair the model learned across a removed
function word never occurs at scoring time. Every filing is scored by
the same rule, so the difference is a fixed feature of the measure
rather than a source of bias between filings. On a sample of 37{,}124
descriptions from every class, the two tokenizations give the same
dominant theme for 88\% of descriptions and theme mixes that differ by
a median of 0.07 in total variation (on a 0--1 scale).} \emph{How many
themes.} The number is set by the analyst, and we use fifty.

Fifty is coarse --- roughly one theme per Nice class --- and it is chosen
on three grounds. First, at fifty every theme is a coherent word list. A
standard check is whether a theme's ten leading words actually appear in
the same descriptions more often than chance would put them there
(normalized pointwise mutual information, which runs from $-1$ to $1$, with
$0$ meaning no association; \citealp{Lau2014}). At fifty themes the average
is $0.48$ and no theme falls below zero. At 200 themes the average falls to
$0.33$ and 11\% of themes fall below zero; at 500 themes, to $0.23$ and
21\% --- a fifth of the finer model's themes are word lists whose words
rarely occur together. Finer models fit the text more closely, but a
closer statistical fit does not by itself make themes more interpretable
\citep{Chang2009}. Second, the themes themselves come back when the
model is refitted. Refitting with a different random seed and matching
each theme to its closest counterpart, the median theme's word list has
a cosine similarity of $0.74$ with its match at fifty themes, against
$0.54$ at 200. The per-filing scores are a noisier matter at any
resolution: in the software class a second seed reproduces lead at
$r = 0.72$ at fifty themes ($0.79$ at 200), and moves the five-year-proof
contrast from $+3.2$ to $+3.9$ points. Each fit is therefore a noisy
measurement of lead, and noise of this kind pulls estimates toward zero
rather than creating them. Third, and decisively, the findings do not
depend on the choice. Section~\ref{sec:three_scorings} rescores every
registration under 500 global themes and under 50 themes fitted
separately inside each class --- 2{,}250 class-specific themes in all, the
finest partition tested --- and the headline contrast is $+2.3$, $+2.6$ and
$+1.9$ points under the three, and $+2.2$ at 200 themes (Supplementary
Material, \S S2 and \S S4). Fifty is the resolution at which the themes
are easiest to read and every result holds; nothing in the paper rests on
the model having found the one right number.

This build --- fifty global themes, per-filing five-year windows, flat
weighting --- is the production scoring, used for every estimate unless
the text states another. The themes are shared across classes; what is
specific to a class is the reference, the average theme mix of that
class's own filings in the window. The Amazon filing draws on six themes
at more than a twentieth of its weight: $0.41$ on media and entertainment
goods, $0.15$ on computer and information services, $0.13$ on retail
store services, $0.10$ on printed matter, $0.09$ on downloadable and
online software, and $0.07$ on marketing of consumer goods.

\paragraph{Comparing against the two references.} Write $P$ for the
filing's distribution over themes, and $Q^{-}$ and $Q^{+}$ for that
distribution averaged over every same-class filing in the two windows.
How far $P$ sits from a reference $Q$ is the Kullback--Leibler
divergence,
\[
  \mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, Q) \;=\; \sum_{k} P_k \log \frac{P_k}{Q_k},
\]
zero when the two distributions agree and growing as the filing puts
weight where the industry puts little, measured in nats. Write
$K^{-} = \mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, Q^{-})$ and
$K^{+} = \mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, Q^{+})$: the filing's past-facing and
future-facing surprise, what the first reader feels and the second.

\paragraph{Taking the difference.} Amazon's filing is scored against
41{,}166 earlier filings and 146{,}532 later ones, giving
$K^{-} = 1.286$ and $K^{+} = 1.163$. The two axes used throughout are
their average and their signed difference:
\[
  \underbrace{A \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\left(K^{-} + K^{+}\right)}_{\text{atypicality}},
  \qquad
  \underbrace{L \;=\; K^{-} - K^{+}}_{\text{lead}} .
\]
For Amazon, $A = 1.225$ and $L = +0.123$: against its own class, its
atypicality is at the 47th percentile and its lead at the 94th. The
industry moved toward this description in the five years after it was
filed and had not been writing that way in the five years before.

Why the average and the difference, rather than the two surprises
themselves? Because $K^{-}$ and $K^{+}$ correlate at $0.99$: a filing
far from its class's past is almost always far from its future too, so
entering both is close to entering one variable twice. The average
collects that large shared component and the difference collects the
small residual that carries the timing; nothing more than a sum and a
difference is taken, with no weights estimated. The two resulting
axes are only weakly related: within class, lead and atypicality
correlate at $-0.10$ across all scored filings, though the figure varies
by class, from $+0.04$ in software to $+0.25$ in advertising and retail,
and Section~\ref{sec:three_scorings} checks that the lead results do not
run through atypicality. The cost of the construction is that $L$ is a
small residual of two large quantities, with about a seventh of either
level's spread, which the supplementary material (\S S4) quantifies.

\paragraph{Why the words are not scored directly.} The words themselves
could be compared to the reference, and the paper reports that scoring
as a cross-check. It cannot be the primary one, because a divergence
measured on words mostly counts the length of the filing. For any two
distributions,
\[
  \mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, Q) \;=\; \underbrace{-\textstyle\sum_k P_k \log Q_k}_{\text{how unexpected these words are}}
  \;-\; \underbrace{\left(-\textstyle\sum_k P_k \log P_k\right)}_{\text{how spread out the filing is}} ,
\]
and the spread of a filing over words is capped by how many distinct
words it uses: a description with $k$ distinct terms cannot spread past
$\log k$, while its class's reference spreads over thousands. A short
description is therefore charged for its narrowness before any of its
content is considered, and the shorter the description, the larger the
charge. In the data, term-scored atypicality correlates with the log of
a filing's distinct-term count at $-0.65$ in the software class and
$-0.64$ across all classes (within class). Themes break the coupling
because all 50 coordinates are always occupied: theme-scored atypicality
correlates with the same count at between $-0.10$ and $+0.10$. What
words can show and themes cannot is specific new wording --- a word
pair like Amazon's \emph{line search} has no place in any theme --- so
theme scoring carries every estimate, and the worked examples in
Supplementary Material \S S2 trace three filings through both
representations.

\paragraph{What the themes see, and what they do not.} The score reads
which themes a filing draws on and how heavily, not how it combines
them: on a 150{,}000-filing software-class sample, the rarity of a
filing's two leading themes explains 70\% of the variance in
atypicality and the unusualness of the pairing adds under two points. A
filing assembled from ordinary parts in a genuinely new configuration is
close to invisible to this measure. Scoring arrangements directly ---
a pair-level lead, and word-level recombination in the sense of
\citet{Fleming2001, Uzzi2013} --- shows the two channels acting in
opposite directions in the software class and neither generalizing
corpus-wide, so the construct's blindness to combination is a bounded
omission rather than a hidden bias in one direction (full estimates in
Supplementary Material \S S2).

\subsection{Within class, not across it}

Goods/services descriptions are drafted to secure scope of protection,
and counsel reuse language from the USPTO's own Acceptable
Identification Manual, from house precedent, and from competitors'
successful filings. Identical wording across unrelated firms is
therefore common: 17\% of scored registrations share a value with
another filing in their class and registration year. Within a class, the
measure picks up departure from a shared drafting convention. Across
classes it does not: a class where the Manual supplies dense standard
language shows a compressed distribution of atypicality, and one where
firms describe novel services in prose a wide one, for reasons unrelated
to how innovative either industry is. Every estimate is therefore taken
within class and year, and magnitudes compare across classes only as
signs and orderings. The vocabulary is positional rather than causal: a
leading filing was ahead of its industry's language, which implies a
trajectory without asserting the filing caused it.
