\section*{Where being leading costs: the factors together}

\emph{For the average registration, leading costs 1.1 points of
survival once the factors are held; without them, 2.3.} About half of
the raw penalty comes from who files leading language and what it
describes: leading filings are more often self-filed, more often
platforms, and more often use the vocabulary of general-purpose
technologies, and all three survive less.

\emph{Four factors change the cost of leading after adjustment for
multiple testing.} Counsel removes it: among counsel-represented
registrations leading costs nothing (0.0 points), among self-filed ones
5.0. Platform and network offerings and general-purpose-technology
vocabulary raise it, to 6.1 and 2.9 points. And it has grown over time:
one SD of filing year (about five years) adds 0.8 points. Figure~1 draws
each of these, with the class groups whose difference from all other
classes is also significant.

\emph{Patenting marks a larger, not a more durable, portfolio.} Owners
who patent survive the proof 1.7 points less often, and owners with 25
or more earlier filings 10.3 points less often: large portfolios let
more marks lapse. Among patenting owners leading costs 1.0 point, the
same as among others (difference $+0.1$).

\emph{Booms and busts do not change the cost of leading.} Against years
that were neither, leading costs 0.1 points less in boom years
(1998--2000, 2005--07) and 0.2 points more in bust years (2001--02,
2008--09), neither distinguishable from zero. Without the filing-year
trend both appeared to reduce the penalty, because the years that were
neither are mostly 2010--19, when the penalty was largest. With the
trend held, growth in a class's filing volume taken alone no longer
passes the adjustment ($-0.6$ points per SD, s.e.\ 0.22), but the
pace-of-change quadrants do: with the trend held, leading costs 4.7
points where both the theme mix and the filing volume changed fast
(s.e.\ 0.31), 2.7 where only volume grew, 1.0 in calm class-years and
0.8 where only the mix turned over. What predicts the cost of leading is
fast volume growth, most of all when the mix is also turning over.

\emph{Geography moves survival, not the cost of leading.} Each SD of a
theme's co-location lowers survival by 1.6 points and each SD of growth
from first-time filers by 1.3; neither changes the lead effect, and being
in a hub changes neither survival nor the lead effect.

\emph{Against all other classes, alcohol, tobacco and finance survive
more and pay more for leading.} With only registration-year fixed
effects, registrations in classes 33, 34 and 36 survive 4.4 points more
often than those in all other classes, and leading costs them 2.9
points against 0.4 elsewhere. The composite group where leading costs
most also survives more (5.7 points) while paying 5.1 points for
leading; in the group where leading costs least, leading is worth 7.6
points, but Figure~1 shows the shape is an inverted U: survival rises
from the most lagging decile (41\%) to a peak near the 65th percentile
(53\%) and falls back in the most leading decile (46\%). In those
classes the cost is to lagging, not a reward to leading first.

\emph{Beer and tobacco differ by what their leading filings described.}
In class 32 the lead effect is $+9.6$ points; in class 34, $-12.8$. The
class-34 penalty is confined to registrations of 2013--2018 ($-23.4$;
$+4.1$ and $+0.5$ in 2002--07 and 2008--12), when e-cigarette and vaping
language went from none of the class's registrations to 46\%, and its
most leading theme is the e-cigarette theme. Vaping registrations
survive the proof 15.8 points less often than other class-34
registrations, and within each product --- vaping, cigars, cigarettes,
hookah --- leading is not penalized (point estimates $+6$ to $+25$).
The class-level penalty is a between-product effect: leading language
in class 34 meant vaping, and vaping marks died. In class 32 the rising
product was beer (33\% of registrations in 2002--07, 67\% in 2013--18),
beer registrations survive 7.5 points more often than the class's
others, and leading beer filings survive 10.1 points more often than
lagging ones. The record does not say why vaping marks died; the
timing coincides with federal regulation of e-cigarettes as tobacco
products from 2016 and the premarket review that followed, which is a
hypothesis the data here do not test.
